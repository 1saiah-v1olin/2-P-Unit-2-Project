Pines of Rome

By Isaiah Brown

Pines of Rome is a symphonic tone poem created by Italian composer Ottorino Respighi in 1924 as a part of the Roman Trilogy.

The piece starts in the famous Villa Borghese, depicting children running around, playing, and having fun. There are no basses to show how lighthearted and fun this movement is meant to be played. There are a bunch of children's elements, like sticks clashing as swords, muted trumpets portraying kids pretending to be their own army entering battle, as well as a glockenspiel, a classic kids instrument.

The second movement starts without pause from the 1st movement, immediately transporting the listener into the Roman Catacombs. Unlike the Catacombs in Pictures at an Exhibition, instead of being dark and unstable with heavy brass chords, Respighi depicts these Catacombs as an atmospheric, almost otherworldy experience. The bassoons and horns repeat a broken-down Gregorian chant, portraying people praying in the Catacombs.

The 3rd movement also starts without pause, taking the listener from the world of the Roman catacombs to the Janiculum Hill, depicting the grandeur and beauty of Rome at night. The piano solo at the beginning brings out the magical, but realistic, beauty of the city of Janiculum, and the clarinet solo brings out the peacefulness of this city at night. The end of the movement has a real recording of a nightingale meant to be played on a phonograph, but modern performances have better technology for it and they play it on hall speakers.
The 4th movement brings us to the Appian Way, one of the earliest and most important Roman roads, depicting the return of the Roman legions to the city at dawn. The movement starts almost imperceptible, then the violins set the mood with this lamenting, painful minor 9th. The upper strings and the viola are all practically crying at the same time, until the clarinets come in with a triumphant motif, but it still sounds unsure, as if it doesn't know whether its their army arriving or enemy lines. The english horn comes in with a haunting solo, portraying the Roman army appearing over the horizon. The horns come in shortly after the solo is done, solidifying the fact that the Roman army has returned victorious. Respighi put in 6 offstage brass called buccine, which were supposed to be ancient Roman trumpets, but he noticed that they were already obsolete, so he input 2 flugelhorns as sopranos, 2 tenor horns or trombones as tenors, and 2 euphoniums, bass trombones or baritones as bass. The music swells into a victorious climax as Rome celebrates another victory.
